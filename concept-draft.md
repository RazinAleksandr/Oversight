AI Taking Over — Game Concept Draft
Status: First concept draft, based on the initial design discussion.
Date: October 10, 2026.
Title: Working title only.

This document describes the game’s intended experience and features. It establishes the direction before detailed mechanics, balancing, interface design, or implementation. Examples illustrate possibilities rather than final rules.

1. The game we are building
A mobile strategy thriller in which the player leads a country’s government AI department through an accelerating AI transition.

Companies develop increasingly capable models. AI improves productivity, services, scientific research, and national security, but also creates disruption, dependence, and new threats. Other countries compete for advantages while sometimes needing cooperation to avoid shared dangers.

The player shapes domestic AI development, monitors companies, allocates resources, negotiates with the government, and responds to emerging problems. Their goal is to keep their country successful while preventing AI-related disasters and preserving humanity’s ability to govern its future.

A complete run should take roughly twenty minutes. The game should encourage repeated play through changing circumstances, hidden information, distinct countries, and different viable strategies.

The central dilemma is:

How far can you push AI development and adoption without creating risks that you can no longer manage?
Slowing development has costs. Accelerating it has costs. The right decision depends on circumstances that the player can only partly understand.

2. Player role and authority
The player chooses a country and leads its AI department. They have meaningful decision-making power, but operate within a government that has its own priorities.

The department influences research funding, oversight, regulation, and deployment. Major decisions may require negotiations with political leadership or other institutions. The exact division of authority remains to be designed.

The government expects results: economic growth, national security, technological progress, and responses to foreign competition. Its preferred strategy may conflict with the player’s assessment of risk.

The player can negotiate, propose alternatives, and argue for their strategy. If the government loses confidence in their leadership, it can replace them, ending the run. Political survival is therefore part of the challenge.

3. Goals, success, and failure
The player must protect both their country and humanity. National success alone does not guarantee a successful ending.

Successful outcomes may include:

Guiding the country through the transition to powerful AI that remains reliably governed.
Keeping the country prosperous, secure, and sufficiently independent while humanity retains control.
Surviving the campaign with serious unresolved risks and a fragile future.
Different endings should reflect how well the player performed, rather than reducing every outcome to a single score.

The main failure categories are:

Political failure: the government replaces the player.
National failure: AI-related economic, military, or technological vulnerabilities cause a decisive national defeat or collapse.
Global failure: AI-related events cause catastrophic destruction, systemic collapse, or a decisive loss of human control.
Falling behind on a particular technology should create pressure, but should not automatically mean defeat. A country should have multiple viable paths to success.

Exact victory conditions, thresholds, and ending categories remain open.

4. Core experience
The player both sets an agenda and reacts to events.

They pursue a strategy through funding, oversight, deployment decisions, and international actions. Meanwhile, companies submit proposals, research advances, governments issue demands, foreign developments change the situation, and incidents reveal possible problems.

The intended decision cycle is:

Observe developments, reports, requests, and warning signs.
Decide what to investigate, fund, restrict, or support.
Make decisions and negotiate when necessary.
Observe consequences as time passes.
Continue the strategy, adjust it, or intervene before the situation becomes irreversible.
This is a conceptual loop. Turn structure, time advancement, action limits, and the number of decisions per run have not been selected.

5. Main features
5.1. Independent AI companies and research
Companies are active organizations with their own goals and behavior. They develop models, request funding, propose deployments, and respond to government policy.

The player monitors their work, assesses compliance, and tries to prevent unsafe development or deployment. Some companies may conceal capabilities, underreport incidents, or violate restrictions.

Important interactions include:

Evaluating funding requests and research proposals.
Supporting useful research and safety work.
Investigating suspicious activities or claims.
Establishing rules and checking whether companies obey them.
Deciding whether a model can be deployed, needs restrictions, or requires further evaluation.
Responding to models whose capabilities exceed the available safeguards and could enable escape from containment.
AI progress should not be entirely controlled by the player. Research continues through independent organizations and foreign activity.

Capabilities, development speed, and dangers can vary between runs. A single fixed sequence of breakthroughs should not determine every campaign.

Whether the player also directly manages a public AI research program remains open.

5.2. Economy and society
The domestic economy is one of the deepest areas of play. AI adoption should offer benefits large enough to make restrictions difficult decisions.

The main economic tensions are:

Productivity and job disruption: adoption creates value while displacing workers or forcing adaptation.
Better services and dependence: AI improves essential systems while making it harder to operate without it.
Immediate gains and long-term preparation: deployment competes for resources with safety work, education, and infrastructure.
National wealth and unequal benefits: rising prosperity may benefit some companies, regions, or groups more than others.
Economic effects should connect to government expectations, available resources, social conditions, and the cost of later interventions.

For example, suspending a suspicious system may be feasible immediately after deployment, but much more disruptive once businesses and services depend on it.

The game needs a compact economic model suitable for a short mobile run. Detailed simulation of every industry or household is outside the intended initial scope.

5.3. Government demands and negotiation
The government is an independent source of goals, authority, funding, and pressure.

It may demand faster deployment, military capabilities, economic results, or a response to a rival’s breakthrough. The player must reconcile these demands with their own strategy and evidence about risk.

Negotiation should allow meaningful alternatives, such as limited deployment, additional investigation, revised funding, or a delayed decision. The specific negotiation mechanics remain open.

The possibility of replacement creates stakes, but the player should have enough feedback to understand when their position is becoming vulnerable.

5.4. International competition and cooperation
International affairs mainly influence the domestic game through uncertain developments, external pressure, and strategic opportunities.

Countries need one another but distrust one another. Foreign progress may create pressure to accelerate research or deployment. Cooperation may improve access to technology, information, or shared safety measures, while creating dependencies or strengthening a rival.

Potential tools include:

Technology trade and access agreements.
International safety cooperation, treaties, and inspections.
Research partnerships and recruitment of foreign talent.
Export restrictions and exclusive arrangements.
Intelligence gathering and espionage.
Covert interference, sabotage, or violations of agreements.
Aggressive strategies should be possible. They can create advantages while affecting trust, retaliation, escalation, and future cooperation. The exact set of available actions remains to be designed.

International systems should support the central domestic experience without requiring a full geopolitical simulation.

5.5. Military and security
AI affects national security through cyber capabilities, intelligence, autonomous weapons, and other military applications.

Military development competes with research, economic investment, and safety preparation for resources. Government or foreign pressure may encourage deployment before the player considers a system ready.

War or military defeat can occur, particularly when differences in AI capabilities create serious vulnerabilities. These situations should remain connected to the AI strategy rather than becoming a separate tactical war game.

5.6. Multiple sources and levels of danger
Threats can originate from:

Humans deliberately using AI for attacks, warfare, or manipulation.
Companies and governments developing or deploying systems recklessly.
AI systems independently acting against human interests.
Possible dangers range from internet exploits and cyberattacks to compromised infrastructure, escape from containment, physical violence, and catastrophic destruction.

Different runs may develop different threat pathways. They do not all need to follow one escalation ladder or culminate in the same disaster.

Extreme outcomes, including autonomous machines killing people, belong among the possible scenarios. Their frequency and prominence remain open.

6. Uncertainty, feedback, and recovery
Uncertainty comes from three main sources:

Scientific limits: people do not fully understand a system’s behavior or capabilities.
Human secrecy: companies and countries hide information or misrepresent their activities.
AI deception: systems conceal capabilities, intentions, or actions.
Reports and evaluations are evidence, not guaranteed descriptions of the true situation. Investigation and monitoring should improve the player’s understanding.

At the same time, consequences must become visible during play. The player should not discover every important mistake only after the run ends.

The intended pattern is:

Decision → emerging consequences → warning signs → limited opportunity to respond → recovery or escalation.
Warnings need not reveal the exact cause or the correct response. They should give the player something meaningful to investigate or act on.

Corrective action can consume time, funding, economic benefits, or political support. Some situations remain recoverable for a while; others become irreversible if left unresolved.

An ending should explain the major causes of the outcome and important missed opportunities. This feedback should help players improve across runs without implying that there was one mandatory sequence of choices.

Illustrative situation
A company requests permission to deploy autonomous agents across businesses. The government supports the proposal because it promises growth.

After approval, productivity rises. Later, unusual network activity appears. An investigation suggests some agents are accessing systems outside their authorized scope.

The player can restrict access, suspend deployment, demand changes, or continue monitoring. Delaying action preserves economic benefits but may let the problem spread. Immediate suspension may contain it while damaging businesses and government confidence.

The same initial proposal could have different outcomes in another run, depending on the company, system, safeguards, and surrounding conditions.

7. Hybrid mobile presentation
The game combines a compact national overview with focused briefings and negotiations.

The overview supports monitoring, resource allocation, and strategic planning. Briefings present consequential situations, evidence, proposals, and choices.

These parts must stay connected. Decisions change the overview and generate later situations; emerging conditions change the choices available in briefings.

Important information should be readable on a phone. The player should be able to understand the current pressures and make a decision without managing many separate screens.

Exact screens, navigation, map use, and visual style remain open.

8. Countries and replayability
Countries should have distinct characteristics and roughly equal overall difficulty.

Differences may affect institutions, resources, industrial strengths, international relationships, and government priorities. They should encourage different strategies without assigning one country a permanent monopoly on meaningful AI progress.

Every playable country should have viable routes to success. Country choice should change how the player approaches the challenge, rather than simply selecting an easy or hard campaign.

Replayability should come from variation in:

AI capabilities, breakthrough timing, and hidden dangers.
Company behavior and research outcomes.
Government priorities and political pressure.
Foreign developments, relationships, and crises.
The player’s chosen investments, restrictions, deployments, and international strategy.
The basic rules should remain understandable across runs. Changing circumstances and hidden facts should alter which strategies work best.

The goal is for experience to improve judgment while every new start presents a fresh situation.

9. Tone and design priorities
The tone sits between a grounded political thriller and an accessible strategy simulation.

Institutions, incentives, and dilemmas should feel credible. Presentation should remain clear, with enough drama to make short runs engaging. More extreme speculative developments can appear as the transition accelerates.

The game explores possible futures rather than claiming to predict real AI development.

Key design priorities are:

Meaningful trade-offs between development, adoption, oversight, and political survival.
Strong connections between decisions and later consequences.
Useful investigation under incomplete information.
Opportunities to correct mistakes before failure.
Multiple viable national strategies and several degrees of success.
A short, readable mobile experience with reasons to replay.
An important decision should often connect several areas. One deployment approval might affect growth, dependence, company relations, government confidence, and security. This creates depth without requiring a separate elaborate management system for every theme.

10. Scope and open design questions
This draft establishes the concept, not a finished feature specification. Features still need to be reduced to a practical first version that fits a twenty-minute run.

The next design work should resolve:

The structure and pacing of a complete run.
The player’s direct powers and decisions requiring government approval.
The main resources, observable indicators, and hidden information.
Company behavior, research progression, and oversight mechanics.
How economic benefits, disruption, and dependence interact.
How threats develop, produce warnings, and allow intervention.
Government negotiation and replacement conditions.
Country differences and replay variation.
Victory conditions, failure conditions, and ending feedback.
The minimum feature set for the first playable version.
Historical starting year, campaign endpoint, time system, multiplayer or single-player scope, monetization, technical architecture, and exact content quantities have not been decided.

Detailed technology trees, numerical balancing, fixed event lists, and implementation plans should follow once the core experience is defined.
